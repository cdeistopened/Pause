If psychedelic medicines are being held out as legitimate medicines which both heal people and raise consciousness, then it is essential that the psychedelic community, scientists, and practitioners demonstrate this higher consciousness through practicing the highest standards of transparency about what is known, what is not known, and what remains uncertain about these substances.

In order to bring you the most up-to-date science-based information, I have interviewed the most prominent psychedelic scientists in the United States and England. Much of what you'll read comes from these one-on-one conversations with the researchers themselves.

An adverse effect is an undesired or harmful effect. Many activities in life bring with them adverse effects or the potential for such effects. When deciding whether to take any action, we can either make a considered decision weighing the risks and rewards, or we can throw caution to the wind. How much consideration we give typically relates to the possibility of serious harm. Not that we always act sensibly, but it makes sense that greater risks call for deeper reflection.

The most severe adverse effect, of course, is death. Years ago, we were to dine with a visiting professor at the University of California, Berkeley, but missed the dinner. Sometime later we learned that the professor died at the restaurant dinner table due to an allergic reaction to peanut oil. On a less dramatic scale, some people, like my lovely wife Jolee, are allergic to cats but willing to pet them, accepting itchy eyes because the enjoyment of the petting is worth the discomfort. I once took a cardiac medicine, spironolactone, but stopped when it blistered my lips - the adverse effects far outweighed any potential benefit.

Stories of adverse effects are common in all cultures - they're one way that humans learn. Against today's backdrop of sobering statistics, these stories take on new urgency. In 2022, approximately 480,000 people died from smoking cigarettes. Another 140,000 died from the adverse effects of alcohol. The toll from pharmaceutical drugs is equally stark: 70,000 from fentanyl, 17,000 from oxycontin, 27,000 from cocaine, 14,000 from heroin. Meanwhile, deaths from classic psychedelics like LSD, psilocybin, MDMA and Ayahuasca remain vanishingly rare.

This data informs our participation in what has become a worldwide experiment with psychedelic medicines. As research accelerates and personal experimentation grows, we must ask: what are the rewards, the risks, the adverse effects? From my perspective, it appears a significant percentage of the American public have been misled about the relative dangers of these substances. While we should remain cautious and informed about all psychoactive compounds, the data suggests some of our societal fears may be misplaced.

Until we collaborate as a people in placing the value of life above the value of money, we all suffer from what I call Honest Information Deficit DisEase (HIDDE, pronounced "HIDDY"). We often cannot trust what we get from cigarette, alcohol and pharmaceutical companies, and even some academic institutions, as profit motives can override public health concerns.

In an effort to offer a glimmer of light in these murky waters, I advocate that psychedelic science set the model for radical transparency. This book represents a step toward that goal.

For nearly sixty years, when reflecting on my traditional scientific self-experimentation with psychedelics, I saw primarily growth, expansion, and creativity. These medicines helped me create a life with meaning, love, and little fear. They facilitated my living in a state of chronic gratitude, fostering an endeavor to treat every person I encounter with dignity, respect, kindness, and love.

Yet in writing this book, in focusing keenly on the multi-dimensionality of adverse effects, I've come to recognize consequences I had previously overlooked. Two in particular deserve special attention.

One adverse effect I suffered from self-experimentation with psychedelics is what I call Government Induced Guilt Syndrome (GIGS). The paradigm is simple: when elected officials criminalize behavior that a significant percentage of the population legally enjoyed, every person continuing that behavior becomes, instantly, an outlaw. Those of us committed to living within the Constitution and its laws, who disagree with but continue the now-illegal behavior, suffer the adverse effects of this syndrome. We think of ourselves as good, honest citizens, yet carry the weight of knowingly breaking the law.

I first took LSD in 1965 while it was legal. I had read Leary and Alpert's book, The Tibetan Book of the Dead, where they noted that eating 400 Heavenly Blue or Pearly Gates morning glory seeds would produce "a full LSD experience." A dear friend, Alan Pinsince, and I set the stage, brought along our female partners for guides, and managed to each swallow 400 seeds as prescribed. No small feat getting that many seeds down the esophagus.

The experience was monumentally positive, along with terrifying moments that became exponential teachers. But as soon as LSD became illegal, despite continued positive experiences, I began living with a guilt instilled by doing something wrong because it was illegal. This emotional burden triggered old childhood trauma of similar nature.

I do not continue to experiment with Ayahuasca, after 3 experiences, in part because it is an emetic and causes 68% of those who take it to suffer the adverse effect of regurgitating. I am one of them. Voluntary projectile vomiting is just not my cup of tea.

The second adverse effect I've identified is Danger Fearlessness Syndrome (DFS). This occurs when someone has so thoroughly conquered their fear of death that they miss weighing risk and reward in life-jeopardizing activities. My own DFS led me to take up motorcycling in my mid-40s, contributing to a head-on collision with a Winnebago that ran over me and crushed both legs. While my psychedelic-assisted awareness training likely helped me maintain presence post-crash, saving my life and legs, the incident demonstrated how the great benefit of living without fear carries its own dangers.

These two syndromes - GIGS and DFS - might seem far from what we typically consider adverse effects of medicines. But they deserve their place because they're quite real, and they might inspire you to consider unknown adverse effects in all kinds of ways of being and living.

The adverse effect of skin cancer, which I endure, stems from outdoor activities I engaged in 50 and 60 years ago. An adverse effect of drinking one soda pop daily for three years is a weight gain of nearly 47 pounds. With Americans averaging 1.25 bottles daily, this simple habit makes a major contribution to our national obesity epidemic. These connections between action and consequence often become clear only in retrospect.

Looking back, there are actions I would not now take when more carefully considering rewards against potential adverse effects. I've paid heavy tuition by experiencing adverse effects, learning lessons the very hard way. There were times when I risked my life for what could reasonably be considered frivolous reasons. I believe this reckless behavior stemmed from what I call Drug Induced Grandiosity (DIG) - a fog that clouded my clarity of mind and supported a sense of immortality. Many readers will recognize this feeling of immortality during and after psychedelic experiences. It's a phenomenon worthy of much deeper examination.

I imagine you agree that the decision to take a medicine known to be dramatically powerful deserves our careful attention. As informed citizens, we want to understand both benefits and liabilities before ingestion. We seek information from the internet, pharmaceutical companies, pharmacies, health professionals, media, family, and friends. We ask around: who might have taken this? Yet in a world where profit often trumps public health, which sources can we trust?

This book strives to illuminate both the promise and perils of these powerful substances. Through careful examination of known risks, emerging research, and personal accounts, we can work toward more informed decisions about their use. Only through radical transparency - about both benefits and adverse effects - can we hope to realize the full healing potential of psychedelic medicines while minimizing harm.

Let us proceed now to the body of this book, which I hope you will find beneficial in some way.
