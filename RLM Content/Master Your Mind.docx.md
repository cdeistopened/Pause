# **Chapter 1: Your Mind Is a Tool, Not Your Boss**

Your mind is generating thoughts right now as you read this sentence. In fact, your mind is probably generating thoughts about the thoughts it’s generating.  
This endless mental chatter—the voice that narrates your life, judges your decisions, and keeps you awake at 3 AM—isn’t you. It’s a tool. And like any tool, it can serve you or enslave you.  
The mind knows how to turn itself on, and when it does, it wants to be the boss. It wants to present words, thoughts, pictures, music, and more whenever it feels like doing so.  
Most people live at the mercy of their mind’s ability to generate an endless stream of information, which it will do, anytime and anyplace. They wake up to mental chatter, navigate their days dodging thought-bombs, and fall asleep wrestling with tomorrow’s imagined disasters. They’ve never learned they could be the boss instead of the employee.  
Each of us can learn how to both direct the mind and how to turn it off.  
The skills I’m about to share have been refined through decades of one-on-one work with people who needed results, and often needed them fast—executives facing career-defining decisions, surgeons who couldn’t afford mental static during operations, entrepreneurs whose racing minds threatened their success. These are real-life-tested techniques that work when everything is on the line.  
As you’ll read in a moment, these tools even saved my life.

## **A Life or Death Choice**

At age 50, on a sunny Sunday morning, I was riding my motorcycle along the Pacific Coast Highway, in northern California enjoying the freedom of the open road. The sky was clear, the ocean breeze refreshing. It was the perfect day—until it wasn’t.  
I was on a two-lane highway, coming into a descending radius right-hand turn, when a Winnebago recreational vehicle crossed into my lane, hit me head-on, and ran over me. The impact was catastrophic. I found myself lying on the blacktop highway, staring up at the sky, uncertain if I would live or die.  
My consciousness erupted in large flashing red lights—internal alarm bells at maximum volume. Every system in my body was racing, pumping, careening toward shock.   
“This is how people die,” I thought with clinical detachment. Not from the injuries themselves, but from the body’s overwhelming response to trauma.  
I tried to lift my left leg and watched in horror as my lower leg, no longer properly connected to my upper leg, swung up and hit me in the face. In that moment, panic threatened to overwhelm me—the kind of blind panic that shuts down rational thought and sends your body into shock.  
This was a do or die moment. I automatically went into my toolbox, shut off my mind and began conscious abdominal breathing. I directed my entire being to conscious breathing and I stayed with it even as the circle of other motorcycle riders above me were calling, “open up your eyes, open up your eyes.” I continued to consciously breathe until I stabilized my system.  
As I lay there breathing, I saw myself drifting into the great blackness of space. I had the thought that I was dying. I then had a vision of myself, without legs, sitting strapped into a chair, meeting with a patient, and I heard a voice say: 

***“Even without legs, you can still make a contribution.”*** 

I then watched myself drifting back. I opened my eyes, called for a helicopter and then went back to conscious breathing. I did not allow my mind to do anything but stay present.  
And in that critical moment—perhaps the most important moment of my entire life—I applied skills, conscious breathing and mind control, that I had been practicing for years; skills that saved my life which I’ll be sharing with you throughout this book.  
Years earlier, I had trained at the Esalen Institute with some of the world’s foremost breathwork masters and also masters at being in the moment. I had practiced breathing techniques thousands of times. I also learned to separate myself from my thoughts and become the witness to my own mind rather than its subject. I had practiced directing my mind. I repeatedly chose my thoughts, internal videos, and music so that I could do so at will, instantly.   
After being run over, the breath training kicked in automatically. I took control of my breathing. I took control of my internal landscape. I focused my mind. I only created positive solution-oriented thoughts. What I mostly created was blank mind and conscious breathing. I was the boss.  
My training in conscious breathing and mind control made the difference between life and death.  
When the paramedics arrived, they were astonished to find me conscious, coherent, and calm despite catastrophic injuries that should have sent me into shock and killed me. Later, in the emergency room, after doctors had obtained permission from my wife to amputate my legs, this same mental clarity and stable breathing allowed me to advocate for myself and request specific tests before proceeding with amputation.  
The doctor said, “We must move quickly, as you could be bleeding out.”  
I responded, “The crash happened at least an hour ago. If I was bleeding out, I’d be dead by now.”  
He performed the tests and came back surprised, saying, “You are not bleeding internally.”  
I said, “Then we have surgery to do—and not amputation.”  
Fifteen hours of surgery later, I still had my legs.  
I share this story not to impress you with some superhuman ability, but to demonstrate what becomes possible when you train your mind with the same dedication most people reserve for their careers or physiques. The techniques that saved my life that day weren’t exotic or complex. They were simple tools, practiced until they became reflexive—the same tools I’ll be sharing with you throughout this book.  
The difference between those who master these techniques and those who merely read about them isn’t talent or special ability. It’s the commitment to practice—both the tactics and the real-world applications.  
In my case, years of training compressed into the moments that mattered most. But you don’t need to wait for a crisis to begin training. In fact, the best time to develop these skills is right now—when the stakes feel manageable and you can practice with patience rather than desperation.  
Throughout my six and a half decades of clinical practice, I’ve discovered a fundamental misconception that plagues most people: We believe we are our thoughts rather than the thinker of our thoughts.  
This critical distinction between relating to the mind as a tool versus the boss is life-changing. When you believe you are your thoughts, you become hostage to whatever thoughts happen to arise—worries about the future, regrets about the past, self-criticism, anxiety, intrusive images, and mental loops that drain your energy and diminish your life.  
Think about it. How many times have you found yourself hijacked by your own thinking? Perhaps you’re trying to focus on an important task, but your mind keeps generating worries about tomorrow’s meeting.  
Maybe you’re enjoying a friendly dinner with friends, but your thoughts keep circling back to an embarrassing incident from last week, or something you forgot at work, or any number of things, and someone says “where did you go?”  
Or perhaps you’re lying in bed, desperately wanting sleep, while your mind plays and replays scenarios that may never happen and which keep you awake.  
Have you had the experience of making love and enjoying the intimacy, sensuality, ecstasy, when suddenly the mind turns itself on, begins thinking, thinking, thinking, quickly taking you away from the present glory and into the rabbit hole the mind is presenting? End of love making, possibly followed by your partner asking, “what’s the matter, why are you stopping, don’t you find me attractive anymore?”  
This is the mind machine running on autopilot, acting like the boss.  
I went to college for 11 years, earning multiple graduate degrees in psychology including a doctorate. Yet in all that time, I was never once taught how to take control of my mind—how to direct it to think about what I want it to think about or to create the kind of mental movies I want to watch. This critical skill—perhaps the most important skill for mental well-being—was completely overlooked.  
And I suspect it’s been overlooked in your education too.

## **Your Mind Is Simply a Tool**

Here’s the liberating truth: Your mind is simply a tool, not your boss.  
Think of it this way. A hammer is a tool for driving nails. A car is a tool for transportation. A computer is a tool for processing information. Each of these tools serves a purpose, but none of them should run your life. When you’re not using a hammer, you set it down. When you’re not driving, you park your car. When you’re done with your computer, you close it or shut it down.  
But what about your mind? Do you know how to “set it down” when it’s not needed? Can you “park it” when its services aren’t required? Do you know how to “shut it down” when it’s time to be present or to sleep?  
For most people, the honest answer is no. The mind keeps running, generating thoughts, creating images, playing scenarios—often negative ones—regardless of whether this activity is helpful or harmful. And when this mind generation happens, the relationship we have with our mind is backward: the tool is controlling the user rather than the user controlling the tool.  
You may be thinking, “But isn’t this just how minds work? Don’t thoughts just happen to us?”  
This is exactly the misconception I’m addressing. Thoughts don’t just “happen” to us. We are generating thoughts, even when we’re not aware of generating them. And with practice, we can direct this generation process rather than being directed by it.  
As I love to tell my patients: You are the boss of your mind, not the other way around.  
How do you know if your mind is running the show instead of you? Here are some clear indicators:  
**1.Thought intrusions** — Your mind frequently interrupts what you’re doing with unrelated thoughts, often negative or anxiety-producing ones.  
**2.Emotional reactivity** — You experience emotions that seem to come from nowhere, as if they’re happening to you rather than being generated by you.  
**3.Rumination on the past** — You spend significant time mentally revisiting past events, especially embarrassing or painful ones, despite knowing you can’t change them.  
**4.Anxiety about the future** — Your mind regularly creates disaster scenarios about events that haven’t happened, often beginning with “What if...”  
**5.Sleep disruption** — When you try to sleep, your mind becomes more active, replaying the day or planning tomorrow.  
**6.Mental exhaustion** — At the end of the day, you feel mentally tired despite having done nothing physically strenuous.  
**7.Difficulty being present** — You find it challenging to fully engage in the moment because part of your attention is always caught up in mental activity.  
Does any of this sound familiar? If so, you’re not alone. This is the normal state for most people in our society. We’ve never been taught that a different relationship with our mind is possible.  
But it is. And those who learn it—whether through years of trial and error or through guided practice with someone who’s already mastered the terrain—find it transforms everything.

## **Becoming the Observer**

The journey toward becoming the boss of your mind begins with a simple but profound shift in perspective—a moment of recognition that you are not your thoughts but the awareness behind them.  
Try this right now. Close your eyes. Notice the thoughts arising in your mind. Don’t try to change them or judge them—just observe as if you’re watching cars pass on a highway.  
Now, ask yourself: Who is doing the observing?  
If you are your thoughts, who is watching them? The very fact that you can observe your thoughts proves that you are not them. You are the observer, the witness, the awareness in which thoughts appear.  
This recognition—that you are not your thoughts but the awareness behind them—is the first crucial step in establishing your rightful position as the boss of your mind rather than its servant.  
Here I offer an alternative to the Cartesian “Cogito ergo sum”—I think therefore I am.  
My version: “Video, ergo sum”—I observe, therefore I am.  
A powerful first exercise for directing rather than being directed by your thoughts is deceptively simple: counting. Close your eyes and count from one to fifty slowly. When your mind wanders (and it will), gently return to counting. This seemingly basic exercise is profound mind training. By directing your mind to count, you’re practicing being the boss. You’re taking control.  
Make this a discipline—count numbers 5-10 times each day. Throughout my 11 years of higher education, I learned to fill my mind with information but never how to direct it, how to be its boss rather than its servant. The educational system failed me in this regard, just as it has likely failed you.  
After observing thousands of patients over six decades, the pattern is unmistakable: Those who master their minds master their lives. Those who *don’t* master their minds remain at the mercy of mind-generated thoughts, suffering unnecessarily as a result.  
Every day, in every moment, you face a choice: be controlled by your thoughts or control them.

## **The Rider and the Horse**

What does it really mean to be “the boss” of your mind?  
It doesn’t mean forcefully suppressing thoughts or waging constant internal battle. Rather, it means establishing a healthy relationship—like that between a skilled rider and a powerful horse.  
The horse provides tremendous energy and capability, but the rider must maintain direction and control. When rider and horse work together in proper roles, they accomplish remarkable things. But if the horse takes over, deciding where to go and when, the rider becomes merely a passenger on an unpredictable journey.  
Your mind is that powerful horse—magnificent in its capacities for memory, analysis, creativity, and problem-solving. But you must be the rider, maintaining appropriate direction and authority.  
In practice, this looks like:  
•Deciding what to think about rather than letting your mind wander wherever it wants  
•Choosing which thoughts to engage with and which to let pass  
•Directing your mind to focus on solutions rather than ruminating on problems  
•Using your mind when helpful and setting it aside when not  
•Maintaining authority over your mental and emotional states rather than being tossed about by them  
This proper relationship doesn’t develop overnight. Like any significant skill, it requires consistent practice and patience. But even small shifts produce remarkable improvements in quality of life.  
Based on my lifetime of clinical experience and personal journey, the potential benefits are profound:  
**1.Freedom from anxiety** — When you control your thoughts rather than being controlled by them, catastrophic thinking loses its grip.  
**2.Emotional regulation** — You gain the ability to shift emotional states intentionally rather than being at their mercy.  
**3.Improved focus** — Your mind becomes a precision tool that stays on task rather than constantly wandering.  
**4.Better relationships** — You respond thoughtfully to others rather than reacting automatically from old patterns.  
**5.Enhanced performance** — In any field, mental control translates directly to improved capability and results.  
**6.Deeper peace** — The constant mental chatter that disrupts inner quiet comes under your direction.  
**7.Greater resilience** — You bounce back from setbacks more quickly when you control your mental response.  
I’ve witnessed remarkable transformations in people who mastered this fundamental shift. Individuals who suffered from debilitating anxiety for decades found relief when they learned to take the boss position. Couples on the brink of divorce saved their marriages by directing thoughts rather than being driven by reactive patterns. Professionals who had plateaued broke through to new levels when they gained mental mastery.  
In my own life, beyond saving me on that fateful highway—and at other times when I faced death—being the boss of my mind has allowed me to maintain vibrance, purpose, and clarity into my 87th year. Being the boss of my mind has been the foundation for my entire life.

## **Addressing Common Challenges**

As you begin exploring this new relationship with your mind, you’ll likely encounter some predictable challenges. Let me address the most common:

## ***“But these thoughts feel so real and compelling\!”***

Yes, they do—that’s the mind’s power. Thoughts create biochemical reactions that feel utterly convincing. But feeling real doesn’t make them *true* or mandatory. With practice, you’ll learn to recognize the space between thought and reality, where choice lives.

## ***“My mind moves too fast to control.”***

The mind’s speed is precisely why you need to establish authority. Start with simple exercises like counting from 1 to 20 repeatedly. As you build your “boss muscle,” the mind’s speed becomes an asset rather than an obstacle.

## ***“I’ve tried meditation and it didn’t work.”***

Traditional meditation can be valuable, but it’s only one path. The approach I’m advocating is more dynamic and easily integrated into daily life. Rather than setting aside 30 minutes to meditate, engage in multiple 30-90 second counting interventions throughout your day. This distributed practice is more practical and effective for busy people with active lives and minds.

## ***“What if certain thoughts are actually important warnings—won’t these tools suppress real concerns in my life?”***

The mind certainly serves protective functions. Becoming the boss doesn’t mean ignoring valuable signals—it means evaluating them rationally and responding thoughtfully rather than being hijacked. A good boss listens to input but makes the final decisions.

## ***“Is this just positive thinking in disguise?”***

No. Positive thinking tries to replace negative thoughts with positive ones while still assuming you are your thoughts. What I’m describing is more fundamental—recognizing that you are the awareness behind all thoughts. From this position, you can choose which thoughts deserve your engagement.

## **Taking Your First Steps**

The most important step in becoming the boss of your mind is simply recognizing when it has taken control. This awareness alone creates space for change.  
Pay attention to moments when thoughts are running the show—creating anxiety about the future, ruminating on the past, or generating self-criticism. When you notice this happening, remind yourself: “These are just thoughts. I am not my thoughts. I am the observer of my thoughts. I can choose different ones.”  
Here’s a practical application: Every time your mind generates a catastrophic prediction about the future, immediately create an alternative scenario where things go beautifully. Both are imagination—but which serves you better?  
Similarly, when your mind produces self-criticism, counter with three genuine self-compliments. Don’t let criticism stand unopposed. Be the boss and balance the mental landscape.  
This recognition—that you create thoughts rather than being them—is revolutionary. It’s like stepping back from a movie screen and realizing you’re not in the film but watching it. And if you don’t like what’s playing, you can change the channel faster than you can blink.  
Daily practice is essential. Take time to observe your internal dialogue. Listen to the conversations in your head. As you watch, continually remind yourself: I am not my thoughts. I create them. I can create different ones.  
The opportunities for mental mastery are endless, and the only costs are maintaining the physical body you inhabit, and the repetitive brief time investments in learning from someone who’s already walked this path and can help you avoid the common pitfalls.  
In the next chapter, you’ll learn the 60-second technique that I used to save my life on that highway—a conscious breathing tool that works faster than any pharmaceutical, costs nothing, and can be practiced anywhere. This tool has been the foundation of my practice for decades, and it will become the bedrock of your mental mastery.  
Remember: The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step and your first step is recognizing this truth:  
You are not your thoughts. You are the thinker of your thoughts.

# **Chapter 2: The 60-Second Anxiety Antidote**

Of all the tools I’ve gathered in my clinical practice, conscious breathing stands as the most essential. When I was lying on that highway after being run over by the recreational vehicle and crushed—conscious breathing saved my life. Being the boss of my mind came in a strong second.  
When helping patients through panic attacks, major life transitions, or seemingly insurmountable challenges, we always start with conscious breathing because it creates the stable foundation everything else requires.  
In this chapter, I’m going to teach you a breathing technique that works faster than a speeding Valium, costs absolutely nothing, has zero side effects, and succeeds nearly every time. Conscious breathing is the cornerstone of all mental and emotional control.  
I call it the “60-Second Anxiety Antidote,” and once you master it, you’ll possess a tool that can shut down panic attacks before they overtake you, break the cycle of insomnia, stop emotional reactivity in its tracks, enhance your energy when depleted, give you control over impulses that normally control you, and—as I can personally attest—save your life in a genuine crisis.  
But before diving into the technique itself, you need to understand why conscious breathing sits at the heart of what I call the CLEAR pattern—the framework underlying all meaningful change.

## **The CLEAR Pattern: How Real Change Happens**

Throughout six decades of clinical work, I’ve observed that sustainable change is most effectively built by following a road map. Whether someone’s overcoming anxiety, breaking an addiction, or transforming their relationship with their mind, the process must be consistent.  
I call it CLEAR:  
**•C**onsciously breathe  
**•L**ock in intention  
**•E**stablish your plan  
**•A**ct consistently  
**•R**epeat until mastery  
Most people jump straight to action (step 4), or at best, begin with an unconscious intention. They set goals while anxious, make plans while overwhelmed, or attempt change while their internal world resembles a category 5 hurricane. This approach almost guarantees failure.  
Think of trying to perform surgery during an earthquake. Even with the finest surgical skills, the best instruments, and the clearest intentions, you simply cannot operate effectively when everything shakes. The same principle applies to personal change. When your internal state churns with anxiety, stress, or emotional chaos, clear thinking becomes nearly impossible. Decision-making deteriorates. Actions become reactive rather than responsive. Follow-through weakens or vanishes entirely.  
This is why stabilization must always come first. And the fastest, most reliable way to stabilize yourself? Conscious breathing.  
I learned this lesson dramatically. After my motorcycle accident, as I lay on the highway with my legs destroyed, my body’s alarm systems screaming, I had, at most, 90 seconds to stabilize myself before shock would take over. No time for lengthy meditation. No opportunity for complex techniques. Just breath—conscious, directed, life-saving conscious breath.  
The paramedics later told me that most trauma victims in my condition arrive either dead or in full shock, blood pressure plummeting, consciousness fading. They found me calm, coherent, and physiologically stable. The difference? The conscious breathing that shifted my entire system from chaos to control.

## **Your Body’s Hidden Control Panel**

Your breath represents something unique in human physiology—a function that operates both automatically and under conscious control. To understand why this matters, imagine your body as a sophisticated vehicle with both automatic and manual transmission options.  
Most vital functions run on permanent autopilot. Your heart beats without your input. Your liver processes toxins while you sleep. Your digestive system metabolizes food whether you think about it or not. These systems operate beyond conscious reach for good reason—imagine trying to remember to make your heart beat 100,000 times per day.  
But breathing occupies a special category. While it happens automatically (thankfully, or we’d suffocate in our sleep), we can also take conscious control at any moment. This dual nature makes breath your gateway to influencing deeper systems typically beyond reach.  
When you understand the mechanics, the power becomes clear. Your nervous system divides into two main branches that work like your body’s gas and brake pedals. The sympathetic nervous system—your gas pedal—activates when you need quick energy, face threats, or experience stress. It triggers what we call fight-or-flight: heart racing, muscles tensing, breath becoming rapid and shallow.  
The parasympathetic nervous system—your brake pedal—promotes restoration. It manages rest, digestion, recovery, healing, and calm alertness. When active, your heart rate slows, muscles relax, and breathing deepens naturally.  
Here’s the crucial insight: while you cannot directly command your heart to slow or your stress hormones to decrease, you can easily control your breath. And your breath directly influences which nervous system branch dominates.  
Slow, deep breathing immediately activates your parasympathetic system—your brake pedal. Rapid, shallow breathing triggers sympathetic activation—your gas pedal. By consciously controlling your breath, you literally switch between these systems at will.  
I’ve taught patients to lower their blood pressure in real-time through breathing alone. I’ve seen panic attacks dissolve in 60 seconds. I’ve witnessed insomniacs fall asleep after years of medication dependency. All through understanding and applying this simple biological switch.

## **The Architecture of Breath**

At the center of this system sits your diaphragm, a dome-shaped muscle separating your chest from your abdomen. Most people have heard of it but don’t truly understand its power. Picture an internal umbrella that opens and closes with each breath. When you inhale, this umbrella opens downward, creating space in your chest cavity and drawing air into your lungs. Your belly area naturally expands as the diaphragm descends. When you exhale, the umbrella closes upward, helping push air out as your belly falls.  
This natural pattern—belly expanding on inhale, falling on exhale—represents optimal breathing. But modern life conspires against it. Chronic stress causes us to shift into shallow chest breathing. Poor posture compresses our breathing space. Sedentary lifestyles weaken our breathing muscles. Cultural messages about “sucking in” our stomachs further restrict natural breath flow.  
The result? Most adults breathe below their capacity. They wonder why they feel anxious, fatigued, and mentally foggy. It’s like trying to run a high-performance engine on fumes—the system simply cannot function optimally without adequate fuel.  
But here’s the beautiful truth: no matter how long you’ve breathed poorly, your body remembers the optimal pattern. With gentle practice, you can restore your natural breathing capacity and, with it, your ability to shift your mental and emotional states at will.

## **Mastering Conscious Breath**

Now let’s learn the specific technique that has transformed thousands of lives, including saving my own. Unlike many therapeutic interventions that require extensive training or special conditions, this technique can be practiced and utilized anywhere, anytime, for anyone.

**Step 1: Find Your Optimal Foundation**

Begin by finding a comfortable seated position with your back supported. If you’re reading this while sitting in a chair, you’re already there. Place one hand on your chest and the other on your belly, just below your rib cage. Close your eyes if that feels comfortable, or simply soften your gaze downward.  
Take a moment to notice any obvious tension. Are your shoulders creeping toward your ears? Is your jaw clenched? Are your hands gripping something unnecessarily? Are you curling your toes? Without forcing anything, simply allow these areas to soften. Think of it as setting down a heavy backpack you didn’t realize you were carrying.

**Step 2: Discover Your Diaphragm**

Before we add breath, let’s locate and understand your diaphragm. This step often makes the difference between superficial relaxation and profound state change.  
Without breathing, gently push your belly out as if inflating a balloon. Then pull your belly in as if deflating it. No breath yet—just movement. If this feels strange, that’s normal. Most people haven’t consciously moved their diaphragm since infancy.  
Repeat this expansion and contraction at least ten times. You’re awakening muscles that may have been dormant for decades.   
Now we add breath to movement. As you inhale, allow your belly to expand gently. As you exhale, let your belly fall. Keep your chest relatively still—the hand on your chest should barely move while the hand on your belly rises and falls rhythmically.  
Think “small and gentle” rather than “deep and forceful.” We’re creating waves, not tsunamis. Many people try too hard at first, creating tension while attempting to relax. If you notice strain, reduce your effort by half, then half again.

**Step 3: Find Your Natural Rhythm**

Before trying to change your breathing pattern, spend a moment simply observing what’s already happening. Which hand moves more as you breathe naturally? Where does your breath feel smooth, and where does it feel restricted?   
Now explore extending your exhale slightly. Perhaps inhale for a count of four and exhale for six. Or simply notice that your exhale naturally wants to be longer than your inhale. This extended exhale activates your parasympathetic nervous system—your body’s brake pedal—creating an immediate sense of calm.  
Continue this gentle breathing for 30 to 60 seconds. That’s it. No marathon sessions required. Remember: a little over time is a lot.   
The goal is ease, not perfection.

## **Recognizing Success and Navigating Challenges**

How do you know you’re practicing correctly? Your body provides clear feedback. Physically, your belly hand should move more than your chest hand. Your shoulders remain relaxed, not hunching with each inhale. Your jaw stays loose, not clenched with effort.  
Mentally, the breath feels easy and natural, without strain or forcing. The sound of your breathing stays quiet—if you’re breathing loud enough for others to hear, you’re probably trying too hard.  
Let me address the most common challenges people face, because forewarned is forearmed:  
If you feel dizzy or lightheaded, you’re taking breaths that are too large or too rapid. Scale back immediately. Take smaller breaths, slow your pace, and if needed, return to normal breathing before trying again with gentler efforts.  
If you can’t feel your belly moving, try lying on your back with a light book placed on your belly. Watch it rise and fall with each breath. This visual feedback helps your brain reconnect with proper diaphragm movement. Once you’ve reestablished the pattern lying down, gradually return to sitting.  
Some people feel self-conscious about their belly expanding, especially if they’ve spent years “sucking it in.” Remember that this is a private practice—no one’s watching or judging. Your health matters more than maintaining an artificially flat stomach. Besides, proper breathing actually strengthens your core more effectively than chronic tension.  
If you notice you’re creating tension while trying to relax—a surprisingly common paradox—scale back your efforts dramatically. Less is more. Use smaller, easier breaths. Don’t force air in or out. Think “gentle waves” rather than “powerful breathing.”  
When your mind wanders (and it will), simply acknowledge the wandering without judgment and return focus to your breath. If counting helps maintain focus, use it. If counting creates stress, abandon it. Some people prefer shorter sessions of 15-20 seconds repeated more frequently rather than longer sessions. Find what works for you.  
Here I want to introduce one of my most important principles, one that applies to breathing practice and all personal transformation: A Little Over Time is A Lot.

# **Chapter 3: Witnessing Your Thoughts**

“Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom.”

Viktor Frankl wrote these words after surviving Nazi concentration camps, developing logotherapy, and authoring *Man’s Search for Meaning*. He discovered something profound in humanity’s darkest circumstances: even when everything external is taken away, we retain the power to choose our response.  
This space—this gap between what happens to us and how we react—is the territory we explore in this chapter. When you learn to witness your thoughts rather than immediately identifying with them, you create this crucial space. And with it comes the freedom to choose your response rather than being driven to react.  
Think of the last time someone said something that upset you. Did words fly out of your mouth before you knew what you were saying? Did anger flash through your system like lightning, leaving scorched earth in its wake? Or were you able to pause, notice your emotional response arising, and choose a more measured response?  
For most people, reactions run on autopilot. Something happens, and before they can blink, they’ve said or done something driven entirely by that initial emotional surge. They’re passengers in their own lives, carried along by every thought and feeling that arises.  
But it doesn’t have to be this way.

## **The Foundation of Mental Freedom**

What exactly do I mean by “witnessing” your thoughts?  
Witnessing is the act of observing your thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations as if from a slight distance—not to judge them or change them, but simply to notice them.   
It’s the difference between drowning in the ocean and watching waves from the shore. The waves are the same, but your relationship to them transforms everything.  
This subtle shift from “I am angry” to “I notice I’m experiencing anger” creates psychological space that changes how you relate to everything in your mind. Instead of being swept away by every thought that enters consciousness, you can observe them like clouds passing through the sky—present but not permanent, real but not defining.  
When you witness, you simply observe thoughts and emotions without rejection or entanglement. You’re sitting safely on the riverbank watching the water flow by, rather than being swept away in the rushing current. The river keeps flowing—thoughts keep coming—but you’re no longer at their mercy.  
This capacity to witness connects directly to the CLEAR pattern we explored in the last chapter. Remember our five essential steps:  
**1.C**onsciously breathe   
**2.L**ock in intention   
**3.E**stablish your plan   
**4.A**ct consistently   
**5.R**epeat for mastery  
Without the ability to observe our thoughts and emotions, we remain at their mercy—tossed about by every fear, worry, and reaction that arises. We’re trying to navigate life from inside the storm rather than from the calm eye at its center.  
Witnessing represents the bridge between the first two steps of our CLEAR pattern. Once you’ve centered yourself with conscious breathing (C), witnessing helps you lock in intention (L) by creating the space to choose your response rather than react automatically. You cannot set a clear intention while you’re completely identified with every thought that arises.  
Consider this: if your mind is completely identified with the thought ***“I’m a failure,”*** how can you possibly make clear decisions or take effective action? You’re not thinking about failure—you *are a failure* at that moment. But when you can witness that thought:

***“I notice I’m having the thought that I’m a failure.”***

Now, you have options. The thought exists, but it doesn’t own you. 

## **The Practice of Witnessing**

The practice of witnessing is deceptively simple. You don’t need a meditation cushion, a guru, or a weekend retreat. You simply need to pause and observe the machinery of your own mind in action.   
Here’s how to begin.  
Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Focus on conscious breathing for a few moments to settle your system—remember, we always start with stabilization through conscious breathing. Then shift your attention to simply noticing whatever thoughts arise. When a thought appears, mentally note it without judgment or analysis. Like a scientist observing an experiment, you’re gathering data, not writing editorials.  
“Thinking about work.” “Planning dinner.” “Worrying about tomorrow.” “Remembering that embarrassing thing from high school.”  
Then return to watching, waiting for the next thought to bubble up from the depths.  
As you practice, several revelations typically emerge. First, thoughts arise continuously and often randomly, appearing without your conscious direction like bubbles rising in champagne. You don’t think them—they think themselves.  
Second, most thoughts are reruns. The same concerns, plans, memories, and fantasies circle through your mind like a Netflix series on endless repeat. Once you notice this, the tyranny of thoughts begins to weaken. How important can that worry be if your mind has been playing it on loop for the past decade?  
Most importantly, you’ll learn viscerally what I’ve been telling you: You are not your thoughts. You are the awareness in which thoughts appear and disappear. You’re the sky, not the weather. The screen, not the movie. The witness, not the witnessed.  
As I tell my patients: “Take time every day to watch your thoughts and your internal commentary. Watch. Listen to yourself. Close your eyes while listening to the conversations going on inside your head. Make mental note of the tone of voice you’re applying to your thoughts.”  
This practice, done for just 2-3 minutes several times throughout your day, builds the witnessing capacity that enables genuine mental freedom. It’s like developing a new sense—once you can witness, you can never fully lose yourself in thought again.  
This is why witnessing is essential for step two of CLEAR—locking in intention. When you can observe the thought “I’m anxious about tomorrow’s presentation” rather than being consumed by anxiety, you create space to set a clear intention: “I choose to approach this with calm confidence.” The witnessing creates the pause; the intention directs what comes next.

## **Thought Patterns That Drain Your Life Force**

While all thoughts benefit from witnessing, certain patterns particularly deserve your attention. These mental loops drain energy, create suffering, and keep you trapped in cycles that may have been running since childhood. Once you learn to spot them, their power diminishes dramatically.

**The Past Trap**

The past is a minefield of regrets, resentments, and rumination. These thoughts replay events that are literally impossible to change: “I should have said...” “Why did I do that?” “If only I had...” “How could they have...”  
I’m blunt with my patients: The past is done. Finished. Over. You cannot remake yesterday any more than you can unbake a cake. When you spend mental energy trying to rewrite history, you’re pouring your life force into a black hole that gives nothing back.  
The mind loves the past because it feels productive—like you’re solving something or learning something. But notice how you can replay the same scene ten thousand times without anything changing. The past is seductive precisely because it’s safe. Nothing new can happen there. It’s all known, all settled. But life happens now, not then.  
When you catch yourself time-traveling backward, simply note: “Thinking about the past.” Then ask yourself: Am I replaying fond memories for pleasure? Am I genuinely extracting a lesson I can apply going forward? If not, it’s time to return to the present—the only moment where your life is actually happening.

**Future Horror Movies**

If the past is a trap, the future is a horror movie studio in your head. These thoughts create present-moment suffering by screening disasters that haven’t happened: “What if I lose my job?” “What if they reject me?” “What if I fail the test?” “What if, what if, what if...”  
I call this catastrophizing, and it's an epidemic in our anxiety-soaked culture. Whether you’re spinning tales of financial ruin, relationship disasters, health crises, or social humiliation, you’re creating real biochemical stress about imaginary events. Your body can’t tell the difference between a real tiger and an imagined one—both trigger the same stress response.  
Here’s what’s particularly insidious: negative predictions feel responsible, like you’re being a good adult by worrying. But worry has never prevented a single catastrophe. It’s like paying interest on a loan you haven’t taken—all cost, no benefit.  
When you witness yourself directing mental horror movies, label it simply: “Worrying about the future.” Then remember: the present is where your power lives. The future will become the present soon enough. Why poison today with tomorrow’s imagined troubles?

**The Inner Critic’s Greatest Hits**

Self-critical thoughts masquerade as truth: “I’m not good enough.” “I always mess up.” “I’ll never succeed.” “There I go again.” “This is just my luck.” “Why am I so stupid?”  
This internal criticism feels so familiar, so “obviously true,” that we rarely recognize it as just another thought pattern. It’s like background music you’ve heard so long you don’t notice it’s playing. But once you start witnessing, you realize your inner critic has been running the same tired script since you were seven years old.  
Here’s what I tell my patients: “We all have negative thoughts, and they’re dangerous. If you have one negative self-critical thought per day, at the end of the year you’ve had 365 negative thoughts all recorded in your neural pathways. That’s 3,650 in a decade.  
**If you allow one negative thought per day starting at age 15, by the time you’re 45 your mind has recorded 10,950 negative thoughts.**  
No wonder people feel defeated—they’ve been marinating in criticism for decades.  
When you catch self-criticism, don’t fight it or argue with it. Simply note: “Self-critical thought.” Then say one positive thought—or two or three or more. Positive thoughts are recorded and accumulate too.

## **Integrating Witnessing into Daily Life**

While formal practice builds the foundation, transformation happens when witnessing becomes as natural as breathing. This is where the rubber meets the road—or in my case, where the Winnebago met the motorcycle.  
Start by setting up check-in points throughout your day. I recommend beginning with four or five brief pauses where you simply ask: “What’s happening in my mind right now?” These aren’t lengthy meditation sessions—just 30-second reconnaissance missions into your mental landscape.  
Link these check-ins to existing routines. Before you start your car, witness what thoughts are running. While your coffee brews, notice what stories your mind is telling. As you wait for your computer to boot up, observe what mental channels are playing. You’re building the habit of metacognition—thinking about your thinking.  
During these check-ins, you might notice your mind engaged in time travel to the past, producing future horror movies, or running self-critical commentary. Don’t try to stop these patterns yet—just notice them. Label them gently: “Ah, there’s rumination.” “Oh, catastrophizing again.” “Hello, inner critic.”  
This gentle labeling serves multiple purposes. It creates distance between you and the thought. It engages your prefrontal cortex—the part of your brain capable of executive function. And it begins to reveal the repetitive, almost boring nature of most mental content. Once you see your mind has been running the same worry-loops for years, they lose much of their hypnotic power.  
Calm moments are your training camp. The real game happens during emotional turbulence—when anger flares, anxiety spikes, or hurt cuts deep. This is when your witnessing practice pays its biggest dividends.  
When emotions run high, the untrained mind immediately identifies with them: “I AM angry\!” But with witnessing, you maintain that crucial millimeter of space: “I notice anger arising in my system.” The emotion is real, the sensations are real, but you’re not consumed by them.  
This creates what Frankl called the space between stimulus and response. In that space lives your power to choose. Without it, you’re a sophisticated robot running prehistoric programs—someone pushes your button, and you react automatically. With it, you become fully human, capable of responding rather than merely reacting.  
The difference between reacting and responding changes everything. When you react, you’re on autopilot—somebody says something, you answer immediately. Somebody does something, you counter automatically. It’s stimulus-response, no space between. But when you respond, you consider what occurred. You take it in, digest it, and make a conscious decision about your words and actions. That’s the massive difference between being driven by your reactions and driving your own life.  
I’ve watched this single shift save marriages, careers, and lives. The executive who learns to witness his anger before it explodes at his team. Witnessing builds directly on your conscious breathing practice. The breath gives you stability—a home base from which to observe. While your thoughts and emotions swirl like storms, your breath remains steady, anchoring you in the present moment.

## **A Critical Distinction**

One reader wrote to me with confusion about feelings versus thoughts, wondering about their sense of “not feeling good enough.” They asked whether they could control it, fix it, or if they were doomed to carry this feeling forever.

“Not good enough” is a thought, not a feeling. It’s a story your mind tells, not a physical sensation. And like all thoughts, it can be witnessed. Once you see it as a thought rather than an ultimate truth about your identity, its tyranny begins to crumble.  
This distinction matters immensely. Feelings—actual physical sensations—must be felt and acknowledged. But thoughts can be witnessed, questioned, and ultimately chosen. When you mistake thoughts for feelings or facts, you become their prisoner. When you recognize thoughts as mental events that you can observe, you reclaim your freedom.

# **Chapter 4: Changing the Channel**

“If you are thinking and you start to feel uncomfortable, change the channel.”  
As I lay on the Pacific Coast Highway with my legs crushed, having just witnessed the vision of myself in a wheelchair still making a contribution, I faced a critical moment. Instead, I made a decision that would prove as vital as any medical intervention. I changed the channel.  
Rather than fixating on catastrophic damage or overwhelming pain, I redirected my mind to counting breaths. One, two, three, four. When pain intruded, back to one. When fear arose, return to counting. This wasn’t denial—I was fully aware of my situation. But I chose which channel my mind would tune into during those critical moments.  
Later in the emergency room, when doctors sought consent to amputate, I changed the channel again—from despair to determination. I mentally rehearsed the tests I would request, visualized successful surgical outcomes, and maintained absolute focus on recovery rather than loss.  
In the intensive care unit, facing the surgeon’s prediction of permanent wheelchair confinement, I changed the channel once more. Instead of accepting that prognosis, I visualized completing a triathlon within two years. Some might call it unrealistic. I called it choosing a better channel. ( I completed *two* triathlons within 2 years.)  
Each time, the ability to redirect my mental focus directly affected my physiological and psychological response. Controlling what played on my mental screen, helped prevent shock, maintain consciousness, and create the internal conditions that supported healing rather than deterioration.  
This is the power of changing the channel—not mere positive thinking, but deliberate redirection of mental resources toward outcomes that serve rather than sabotage.

## **Taking Control of the Remote**

In the previous chapter, you learned to witness your thoughts—observing them with awareness rather than being swept away. This creates the space where choice becomes possible. But witnessing alone isn’t always enough.  
Sometimes you need to actively redirect your thinking, especially when negative thought patterns are particularly strong, emotional distress is building rapidly, when racing thoughts prevent sleep, you need to focus on a specific task, or you’re caught in loops of rumination.  
These times are when the skill of changing your mental channel becomes essential. Just as you can change channels on a television when an unwanted program comes on, you can learn to redirect your mind when unhelpful patterns arise.  
The beauty of this skill is its simplicity. You don’t need to analyze why certain thoughts arise. You don’t need to fight against them or judge yourself for having them. You simply change the channel, the same way you’d switch from a disturbing news report to something more constructive.  
The simplest and most effective channel-changing technique is counting numbers in your head. This deceptively basic method works because it immediately interrupts thought patterns, requires just enough focus to displace other thoughts, provides clear and unmistakable focus, and is always available—no equipment or special conditions required.  
When you notice your mind dwelling on unhelpful thoughts, immediately begin counting: “1, 2, 3, 4...” Continue steadily, focusing your full attention on the numbers. When other thoughts intrude (and they will), simply return to counting. You don’t need to reach any particular number—the act of counting itself changes the channel.  
Start with just 60 seconds of counting. That’s usually enough to interrupt the previous thought pattern and create space for something new. Think of it like switching channels on a television—you don’t need to destroy the unwanted program; you simply tune to a different frequency.  
Some people worry they’re avoiding important thoughts by changing the channel. But there’s a crucial difference between productive reflection and destructive rumination. If your thinking generates understanding or solutions, it may be worth continuing despite mild discomfort. But if you’re simply replaying old wounds, trying to remake the past, or catastrophizing about the future, it’s time to reach for the remote.  
The key to using this technique wisely is monitoring your emotional state. Your feelings serve as an internal guidance system, telling you when a channel change would be beneficial.  
When reviewing past events or planning for the future, pay attention to how you feel. Rising tension, anxiety, anger, or despair signal that your current mental channel isn’t serving you. You can learn to distinguish between productive discomfort that leads somewhere useful and destructive loops that only deepen suffering.  
Trust your emotional compass. If thinking about a situation leaves you feeling empowered, clearer, or motivated—even if it’s challenging—keep going. But if you’re feeling increasingly hopeless, agitated, or stuck, it’s time to change the channel.

## **Real-World Channel Changing**

Beyond general thought management, the counting technique serves as a precision tool for specific situations where redirecting your mental focus becomes essential.

**Breaking the 3 AM Worry Cycle**

Many people experience what’s called “sleep misperception” or “paradoxical insomnia”—where they believe they’re awake all night despite actually sleeping. This happens because anxiety disrupts our perception of sleep. They experience what I call the “3 AM horror show”—waking in the dark with racing thoughts about everything that could go wrong. Work deadlines, relationship problems, health concerns, and financial worries all seem magnified in the pre-dawn darkness.  
When you find yourself awake at night with racing thoughts, the counting technique provides an ideal channel change. Instead of thinking about how tired you’ll be tomorrow or ruminating on problems you can’t solve at 3 a.m., simply begin counting.  
Rather than counting with the goal of falling asleep (like counting sheep, which creates performance pressure and worsens insomnia), focus on counting simply to redirect your attention away from anxious thinking. Sleep then has the space to arrive naturally.

**Interrupting Rumination Loops**

When you catch yourself repeatedly reviewing past mistakes, embarrassments, or hurts, counting provides an immediate circuit breaker. This isn’t about avoiding necessary processing—it’s about preventing unproductive rumination that only deepens neural pathways of distress.  
You can always return to processing difficult experiences when you’re in a better state for productive reflection. But in the moment when rumination is creating suffering without insight, counting offers a simple channel change that preserves your present moment.

**Managing Social Anxiety**

Before challenging social situations, the mind often creates preview reels of potential disasters. You’ll say the wrong thing. Everyone will judge you. You’ll be exposed as incompetent or boring. Rather than letting this mental movie play unchecked, use counting to redirect your attention.  
A quick 30-60 seconds of counting—in the bathroom before a party, in your car before a presentation, or quietly in your mind during a difficult conversation—interrupts catastrophic thinking and returns you to the present moment where you can engage with what’s actually happening rather than your fears about it.

**Redirecting Pain Attention**

Physical pain often creates a feedback loop with anxious thoughts that amplifies suffering. The pain triggers thoughts like “This will never end” or “I can’t handle this,” which increase tension, which worsens pain, which generates more catastrophic thoughts.  
Counting interrupts this amplification cycle. This doesn’t eliminate physical sensation, but it prevents the mental overlay that compounds suffering. By giving your mind a neutral focus, you reduce the overall experience of distress.

“Isn’t this just avoiding my problems?”

“Something this simple can’t really work.”  
The simplicity is precisely why it works. Complex techniques require bandwidth you don’t have when distressed. Counting works because it’s simple enough to access even in crisis, yet engaging enough to interrupt established patterns. Don’t let intellectual snobbery prevent you from using a tool that works.  
“I forget to use it when I need it most.”  
This is why regular practice matters. By counting for 30-60 seconds many times daily—even when you don’t urgently need it—you build the neural pathway that makes it accessible during stress. To cultivate genuine optimism, take time each day to deliberately set this specific attitude. Go inside, close your eyes, and tell yourself: “I’m choosing optimism. I’m going to see possibilities where others see problems. I’m going to look for solutions, not obstacles.”  
Visualize yourself moving through your day with this orientation. See yourself responding to challenges with curiosity rather than defeat. Feel the energy of “I can figure this out” rather than “This is impossible.”  
But here’s the crucial part: maintain vigilant awareness of what I call “the creep of pessimism.” These subtle negative thoughts try to erode your optimistic foundation like termites in wood:  
*“Oh, I don’t know if I can do that.”*  
*“That kind of thing always happens to me.”*  
*“This probably won’t work.”*  
Notice the negations—don’t, can’t, won’t, shouldn’t. When you catch these pessimistic infiltrators, immediately change the channel to something constructive. This isn’t toxic positivity—it’s choosing thoughts that empower rather than diminish you.  
As with all skills worth developing, this takes “practice, practice, practice, and you will get there.”  
If you doubt your ability to choose emotional states, spend an hour observing young children. Watch them transform from tears to laughter in seconds, shift from frustration to fascination at the appearance of a butterfly, move from anger to affection with stunning agility.  
This emotional fluidity is your birthright. You haven’t lost this capacity—you’ve simply forgotten how to access it. Years of habitual responses have created deep grooves in your neural pathways, making certain emotional patterns feel inevitable rather than chosen.  
But just as you can relearn to ride a bicycle after decades away, you can reclaim your natural ability to shift emotional states at will. The neural circuitry remains intact, waiting to be reactivated through conscious practice. Every time you successfully change your emotional channel, you strengthen these dormant pathways, making the next shift easier.

## **Real Feelings, Conscious Choices**

When I teach emotional mastery, some people worry they’re being asked to suppress authentic feelings or paste on fake smiles. This fundamentally misunderstands the practice.  
Emotional mastery isn’t about denying difficult feelings—it’s about having choice in your emotional life rather than being enslaved by automatic reactions. Sometimes grief is the most appropriate response. Sometimes anger serves as important information. Sometimes fear keeps you safe.  
**The key distinction is between reacting and responding. Reacting is automatic, unconscious, habitual—like a knee jerking when tapped. Responding is chosen, conscious, intentional—like a dancer selecting the next move.**  
When you develop emotional mastery, you can choose to fully experience grief when mourning a loss, rather than having sadness ambush you randomly months later. You can consciously access appropriate anger to set boundaries, rather than exploding over minor frustrations. You expand your emotional repertoire rather than limiting it.

# **Chapter 8: The Power of the Present**

My friend and neighbor, California’s historic Governor Jerry Brown, was once asked by an eager journalist about his legacy. “What contributions do you want to be remembered for?”

The wise Jerry Brown’s response was profound: “My past contributions? I am not looking backward, I am interested in what I am doing now.”  
This answer speaks directly to the fundamental principle of mental mastery I’m sharing in this chapter: the power of the present moment. Even after decades of achievements that most would spend their later years polishing and promoting, Brown’s focus remained firmly in the now—not the past, not his legacy, but what he was doing in the present moment.  
This is the essence of mental mastery that few truly understand. The past is finished. The future hasn’t arrived. The only moment you actually experience is now.

## **The Twin Traps of Time**

I’ve observed that most mental suffering comes from one of two sources: dwelling in the past or worrying about the future. I call these the Twin Traps of Time.

**The Past Trap**

We’ve already talked about how the past is full of traps. It’s full of landmines. It’s full of ways to spoil our now.  
Think about how much time you spend replaying past events in your mind. Conversations you wish had gone differently. Decisions you would make differently today. Embarrassing moments that still make you cringe. Opportunities missed. Mistakes made. Wrongs suffered or inflicted.  
It doesn’t matter whether something happened twenty years ago or this morning. There is nothing we can possibly do to remake the past. The only value in revisiting the past is if you can learn something useful that you can apply now or in the future. In this case we learn from history or are doomed to repeat it. Otherwise, the past is a trap that steals our present moment and has the power to make us miserable.  
The past trap is particularly seductive because it feels natural to think about what has happened. Our culture, influenced by archaic Freudian analysis, encourages us to “process” and “analyze” and “reflect” on the past, as if doing so will make us happier.  
** But figuring out what went wrong does not make one happier; one gets happier by learning skills of being happy.**  
 Most past-focused thinking isn’t constructive—it’s rumination, the mental equivalent of a cow endlessly chewing the same cud, extracting no new nutrition.  
When you find yourself caught in past thinking, remember: the value isn’t in re-experiencing or re-hashing what happened. The only value is in extracting a lesson you can apply today.

**The Future Trap**

During a recent election cycle, I heard from many people who were deeply dismayed about the results. Their minds were filled with worries about what might happen next—politically, economically, socially.  
This future-focused anxiety is the other great trap that steals our present moment.  
Any worry about the future, whether about politics, finances, relationships, health, or a job interview, takes you out of the now. The now is where we are present and where we live. The future trap is especially insidious because it masquerades as responsibility. We tell ourselves we’re “preparing” or “planning” or “being realistic.” But there’s a crucial difference between productive future planning and unproductive predictions of doom.  
Productive future planning is time-limited, solution-focused, and followed by action. Unproductive thinking about the future is worry-focused, problem-focused, and leads to more worry rather than action.  
When you’re genuinely planning, you’re having an intellectual experience in the present moment. You’re thoughtfully considering options and making decisions. When you’re worrying, you’re having an emotional experience of fear about events that haven’t happened and may never happen.  
Yes, thinking about the future right now can be very scary, especially when you predict that something negative will happen. But remember—that future exists only in your mind. The actual future will arrive moment by moment, and you’ll meet it most effectively from a place of present awareness, not anxious projection.  
Now some of you may say, “but what if you are 100% certain that something bad or unpleasant is going to happen?” Knowing something painful is going to happen next week does not justify dwelling on the pain and spoiling the now unless there is actually something you can do to prevent the future pain from happening. That is the only justification.

## **The Reality of Now**

There is only now. There really is no future. There really is no past. There is only now.  
This may sound like spiritual philosophy, but it’s actually practical neuroscience. Your brain can only process information in the present moment. Even when you think about the past or future, that thinking is happening now. The sensations in your body are happening now. Your breath is happening now. Your awareness is always, only, now.  
When we’re fully present, we notice things we would otherwise miss. Presence allows us to be in touch with reality as it actually is, not as we remember it or imagine it might be.  
Allowing your mind to dwell on the past and/or the future can cause anything from inconvenience to debilitating anxiety.  
Here is an example of how thinking and losing the now can cause inconvenience. I enjoy thinking when driving on long trips. One time, on a trip to visit my brother Ed, I got so wrapped up in my thoughts that I missed my turnoff to Tahoe City, California and drove 50 miles before I saw the lights of Reno, Nevada. I had been so lost in mental time travel that I lost track of where I was in space.  
Another example: When my daughter Sarana was 4, we would go on long car rides together from the San Francisco Bay Area to Wilbur Hot Springs and from Wilbur Hot Springs to Tahoe City where she was in junior ski Olympics. There were times when, while driving, I was so lost in thought that I would hear her calling me “earth to Dad, earth to Dad, come in Dad.” I was so in my mind that I was completely out of the present.  
Think about your most meaningful life experiences—moments of great joy, connection, or even calm acceptance during difficulty. Were you fully present during those times? When we’re completely in the now—whether enjoying a sunset, engaged in meaningful conversation, or even navigating a crisis—we experience life most fully.

## **Living in the Now: Real-World Examples**

Four years ago, when I was 82, I was diagnosed with life-threatening melanoma. Two weeks later I was told I was in life-threatening congestive heart failure. My ejection fraction—the percentage of blood the left ventricle pumps with each beat—was a dangerously low 34\.  
Having practiced living in the now for decades, I continued that practice. My wife and I lived every day to the fullest and, other than bringing my Will up to date, gave no thought to the past or the future.  
The only change I made in my daily routine was increasing my aerobic workouts from 45 minutes 4-5 days per week to 60 minutes 6-7 days per week. I completely eliminated the small amounts of alcohol and coffee that I ordinarily ingested. I also began taking prescribed medications for my heart.  
For the nodular melanoma cancer, I had surgery called a sentinel lymphadenectomy. Four days later, Dr. Jonathan George of UCSF called me at 8am on a Saturday morning to tell me that the cancer had never spread. My immune system had built a capsule around the cancer and prevented it from metastasizing. We celebrated and congratulated ourselves, for had we freaked out when we got the diagnosis, we would have spoiled so many “nows” for naught.  
Three months later my echocardiogram showed a normal ejection fraction of 59, and it has remained normal ever since. This year my cardiologist told me I had a normal heart. Once again we celebrated and expressed gratitude for our practiced skill of living in the now. This story has a happy ending, but even if one or both conditions had killed me, Jolee and I would have had the pleasure of all the “nows” until the final event.  
As I shared in an earlier chapter, when my beautiful wife Jolee was diagnosed with breast cancer this year, we again faced a situation filled with uncertainty. We didn’t know what would come next—whether she would need more chemotherapy, radiation, or additional surgery.  
Instead of falling into the time traps, we made the same conscious decision we’d made with my diagnoses: to continue living in the present. We actively avoided spending energy in what I call “the cesspool of the past”—all the things we could have done, might have done, should have done. And we carefully avoided projecting into the future with endless “what ifs.”  
We made ourselves feel good together in the present moment. We thanked one another daily for having each other. We expressed gratitude for food, shelter, warmth, education, and resources. This present-focused approach didn’t change the reality of cancer, but it dramatically changed our experience of that reality.

## **The Practical Path to Presence**

Living in the present moment isn’t just philosophy—it’s a practical skill that can be developed through specific techniques and consistent practice. Here are the core practices:

**The Count-to-Presence Technique**

When you notice your mind trapped in past regret or future worry, immediately begin counting: “1, 2, 3, 4...” Continue counting steadily, focusing your full attention on the numbers themselves. If thoughts of past or future intrude, gently bring your attention back to counting.  
This simple technique works because numbers exist only in the present moment of your awareness. When you count with full attention, you’re automatically in the now.  
Start with 30-90 seconds of counting several times each day. With practice, this brief intervention is often enough to break the grip of time-trap thinking and return you to the present.

**The Sensory Anchor Practice**

Present moment awareness is fundamentally about direct sensory experience rather than mental commentary. To instantly shift into presence:  
•Notice three things you can see, in detail  
•Notice two things you can hear  
•Notice one thing you can feel physically in your body  
•Notice where in your body you have the feeling  
This practice, which takes less than 90 seconds, immediately anchors you in direct sensory experience—the hallmark of present-moment awareness.

**The Present Check-In**

Throughout your day, take brief moments to ask yourself: “Where am I right now—in the past, the future, or the present?” If you find you’re caught in a time trap, use the counting technique or sensory anchor practice to return to the now.  
I recommend doing this check-in at least 5 times daily. Set specific triggers such as before meals, when entering a new environment, or whenever you look at your watch or phone. These regular check-ins gradually build the habit of presence.

**Scheduled Thinking Time**

There is value in thinking. We plan and we create. These are mind activities of value and deserve special allocated time. You have a choice of fitting in this kind of thinking time or scheduling it. This is your decision to make, but be sure to actively make the decision and review it from time to time. The more you take control of such events in your life, the more powerful you will feel and the less like a small boat being thrown around by the ocean of life.

**The Awareness Expansion**

For a deeper experience of presence, try this practice for 90 seconds:  
Begin by consciously breathing and simply noticing the sensations of breathing. Then gradually expand your awareness to include other physical sensations in your body. What do you notice? Next, include awareness of sounds around you. Finally, expand to include visual awareness of your surroundings.  
The key is maintaining this expanded awareness without mental commentary—experiencing sensations, sounds, and sights directly, without labeling or judging them.  
Many people are so unaware of their bodies that they do not notice symptoms until they are in a red zone. One of my cardiologists, Dr. Kent Gershengorn, told me that as many as 30–25% of the ten million Americans who have irregular heartbeats (AFib) are unaware of it. Expanding awareness is life-enhancing.

## **Common Misconceptions About Present-Moment Living**

As I teach these practices, several misconceptions frequently arise:

**Misconception \#1: “Living in the now means never planning for the future.”**

A friend once told me, “I’ve been practicing living in the now that you’ve been educating people about. There’s a lot of value in it. But you know, I didn’t plan for my future. And now I’m concerned that at my age, I’m running out of money.”  
He misunderstood the practice. When you live in the now, you can still engage with future planning as a present-moment activity. When you do plan for the future, that is a now experience—an intellectual experience of making thoughtful decisions.  
Living in the now is meant to be an emotional experience of being fully present with your current activity, surroundings, and the people in front of you. Do you need to spend some of your now time planning for tomorrow? Of course you do. But if you worry about the future and get caught in anxiety about what might happen, then you’re besmirching your now experience.

**Misconception \#2: “Present-moment awareness is about feeling good all the time.”**

The practice of being in the now isn’t about manipulating your emotions to feel perpetually positive. It’s about experiencing whatever is arising in the present moment without getting lost in negative mental commentary about it.  
Sometimes the present contains difficult emotions or circumstances. Being present means experiencing these directly rather than compounding them with worry, judgment, predictions, or resistance. Often, you’ll find that direct experience of even challenging emotions is less painful than the mental suffering created to avoid them.

**Misconception \#3: “I need to empty my mind to be in the present moment.”**

Since having no thoughts remains so difficult for so many, present-moment awareness focuses on changing your relationship with thoughts rather than eliminating them. Thoughts will continue to arise. The practice is noticing them without being carried away by them, especially when they pull you into time traps.
